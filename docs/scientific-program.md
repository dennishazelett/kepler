# Scientific Program

## Purpose

The scientific program defines the intellectual structure of the Kepler project.

Rather than organizing the course around lectures, laboratory exercises, software packages, or statistical methods, Kepler is organized around **investigations**.

Each investigation asks participants to evaluate a historically significant scientific claim using evidence they collect themselves.

The objective is not to reproduce known answers, but to experience the process by which scientific knowledge is constructed.

---

# Philosophy

Scientific knowledge advances by proposing explanations, deriving predictions, collecting observations, comparing those observations with predictions, and communicating reasoned conclusions.

Kepler is designed to recreate this process.

Participants should not simply learn *that* particular scientific claims are accepted.

They should learn *why* those claims became accepted.

Whenever practical, participants should replace belief based on authority with conclusions supported by their own observations.

---

# Investigations

The fundamental unit of the Kepler project is the **Investigation**.

An investigation is centered on a scientific claim that was, or could reasonably have been, believed by careful observers.

Participants are not asked to accept or reject the claim in advance.

Instead, they are asked to determine what conclusions are justified by the available evidence.

Each investigation therefore represents a genuine scientific problem rather than a predetermined exercise.

---

# Structure of an Investigation

Every investigation follows the same general framework.

## 1. Scientific Claim

The investigation begins with a clearly stated claim.

The claim should represent a coherent worldview rather than an isolated fact.

Examples include competing descriptions of the structure or behavior of the natural world.

The purpose is to provide a hypothesis that can be evaluated through observation.

---

## 2. Historical Context

Participants are introduced to the historical circumstances in which the claim arose.

Historical context is not included as background material alone.

It explains why intelligent observers considered the claim plausible and what evidence was available at the time.

History provides the intellectual setting in which the investigation unfolds.

---

## 3. Predictions

Participants ask:

> *If this claim were true, what should we expect to observe?*

Predictions may be qualitative or quantitative.

Different participants may derive different predictions from the same claim.

---

## 4. Observations

Participants collect observations using project instruments and standardized protocols.

Initially these include:

* cross-staff
* quadrant
* gnomon

Observations become part of a shared longitudinal dataset maintained by the project.

Participants are encouraged to combine their own observations with those contributed by the broader Kepler community.

---

## 5. Models

Participants construct models that explain or predict the observations.

Multiple modeling approaches are encouraged.

Examples include:

* geometric reasoning;
* statistical models;
* Bayesian inference;
* machine learning;
* simulation;
* analytical derivations.

No modeling framework is considered privileged.

The purpose is to understand how different models relate claims to observations.

---

## 6. Evidence

Participants compare predictions with observations.

The emphasis is not on proving a claim correct or incorrect.

Instead, participants evaluate:

* what evidence supports the claim;
* what evidence challenges it;
* what uncertainties remain;
* what additional observations would be most informative.

Scientific conclusions are expected to remain provisional and evidence-dependent.

---

## 7. Scientific Argument

Every investigation concludes with a written scientific argument.

Participants should clearly describe:

* the claim under investigation;
* the observations collected;
* the methods used;
* the reasoning connecting evidence to conclusions;
* uncertainties and limitations;
* possible future work.

Communication is considered an essential component of scientific reasoning rather than a final reporting step.

---

# The Role of Data

Kepler is built upon participant-generated observations.

These observations are:

* preserved as immutable records;
* accompanied by complete measurement metadata;
* combined across multiple participants;
* accumulated across multiple course offerings.

The resulting dataset grows continuously as the project evolves.

Later investigations may therefore become possible only after sufficient observations have accumulated.

Participants contribute not only to their own investigations but to future investigations undertaken by others.

---

# The Role of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence is treated as a scientific assistant rather than an answer generator.

Participants are encouraged to use AI to:

* critique experimental designs;
* propose alternative hypotheses;
* identify potential sources of bias;
* suggest additional observations;
* compare modeling approaches;
* improve scientific writing.

AI should support scientific reasoning rather than replace participant observation or judgment.

The primary evidence remains the observations collected by the participants themselves.

---

# Initial Scientific Program

The initial version of Kepler focuses on naked-eye astronomy.

Astronomy was selected because it allows participants to investigate profound scientific questions using inexpensive instruments and observations that remain accessible to nearly anyone.

The initial investigations examine historically significant claims about the organization of the heavens.

These investigations are intentionally presented from the perspective of the historical observers who first confronted them.

Participants are invited to evaluate the claims using their own observations rather than through retrospective acceptance of modern scientific conclusions.

As the Kepler dataset grows, increasingly ambitious investigations will become possible.

---

# Evolution of the Program

The scientific program is expected to evolve.

New investigations may be added as:

* new observational capabilities become available;
* larger datasets are accumulated;
* new analytical methods are developed;
* contributors propose compelling scientific questions.

Each new investigation should remain consistent with the principles established in the Project Charter and Measurement Model.

---

# Enduring Principle

Kepler does not teach conclusions.

Kepler teaches the process by which conclusions are earned.

Every investigation should therefore invite participants to ask not:

> *What am I supposed to believe?*

but rather:

> *What conclusion is justified by the evidence I have collected?*

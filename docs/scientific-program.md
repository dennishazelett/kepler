# Scientific Program

## Purpose

The scientific program defines how Kepler cultivates scientific inquiry.

Rather than organizing learning around lectures, laboratory exercises, software packages, or predetermined experiments, Kepler is organized around investigations of the natural world.

Its purpose is twofold.

First, it develops the practical skills required for careful scientific observation: constructing instruments, making measurements, reasoning about uncertainty, analyzing data, and communicating evidence.

Second, it invites participants to apply those skills to historically significant scientific claims without prescribing how those claims should be investigated.

Kepler teaches the practice of science before asking participants to practice science for themselves.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Philosophy

Scientific knowledge is not acquired by memorizing established facts.

It is constructed through observation, measurement, reasoning, criticism, and communication.

The scientific program is designed to make this process visible.

Participants should not simply learn *that* particular scientific ideas became accepted.

They should learn *why* they became accepted, what evidence supported them, what uncertainties remained, and how reasonable investigators might have reached different conclusions given the observations available at the time.

Whenever practical, authority is replaced with evidence.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Two Modes of Scientific Learning

The scientific program is built around two complementary forms of investigation.

Neither is complete without the other.

# Investigations

The fundamental unit of the Kepler scientific program is the **investigation**.

Kepler recognizes two complementary forms of investigation, each serving a different educational purpose.

Neither is complete without the other.

---

## Foundational Investigations

Foundational investigations develop the habits and skills required for reliable scientific observation.

Unlike instrument calibration or construction activities, foundational investigations focus on observing the natural world itself. They provide guidance while allowing participants to develop intuition about the sky through repeated observation.

Examples include:

- creating a personal star map;
- following the annual motion of the Sun;
- tracking the Moon through a complete lunation;
- locating and following the visible planets;
- measuring the changing elongation of Venus;
- observing the retrograde motion of Mars.

Participants are encouraged to choose investigations that match their interests rather than follow a prescribed sequence.

These investigations build scientific judgment through experience.

---

## Claim Investigations

Claim investigations address historically significant scientific claims.

Rather than asking participants to reproduce a known experiment, claim investigations invite them to determine what conclusions are justified by evidence they collect themselves.

Each claim sheet provides historical context and establishes the evidentiary environment for the investigation, but intentionally avoids prescribing an observational strategy, analytical method, or scientific argument.

Different investigators may legitimately choose different instruments, collect different observations, construct different models, and communicate different conclusions.

This diversity of scientific reasoning is not an unintended consequence of the project.

It is one of Kepler's primary educational objectives.

---

## Scientific Claims

Claim investigations begin with a scientific claim that has been, or reasonably could have been, believed by careful observers.

Examples include:

- The universe is geocentric.
- The Earth is stationary.
- The Earth is spherical.
- The stars are fixed to a celestial sphere.

The purpose is not to recreate history for its own sake.

Historical claims provide intellectually honest opportunities to investigate fundamental questions using observations that remain accessible today.

Participants are encouraged to ask questions such as:

- What observations would be expected if this claim were true?
- What observations would challenge this claim?
- What measurements are possible using the available instruments?
- What additional evidence would strengthen the investigation?

These questions are intended to stimulate investigation rather than prescribe it.

Scientific reasoning begins where prescribed procedures end.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The Role of Observation

Observation is the foundation of every investigation.

Kepler therefore places unusual emphasis on measurement itself.

Participants are encouraged to understand not only what they observe, but how those observations are produced.

Instrument design, calibration, observer variability, environmental conditions, and measurement uncertainty are treated as legitimate subjects of scientific inquiry rather than inconveniences to be ignored.

Reliable conclusions depend upon reliable observations.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The Role of Data

Kepler is built upon participant-generated observations.

These observations are:

- preserved as immutable primary records;
- accompanied by complete measurement metadata;
- collected using openly documented protocols;
- organized according to shared community standards;
- accumulated across multiple observers and multiple investigations.

The resulting dataset grows continuously as the project evolves.

Participants may choose to investigate scientific claims using only their own observations, using community-contributed observations, or by combining both.

Each approach offers different opportunities for scientific reasoning.

An investigator who collects an entirely independent dataset experiences the challenge of designing and executing a complete scientific study.

An investigator who analyzes community observations can address questions requiring broader spatial, temporal, or observational coverage than any individual could reasonably achieve.

Many investigations will naturally combine both approaches.

Community standards for instruments, observations, metadata, and data formats exist to make independently collected observations interoperable.

They standardize measurements—not scientific reasoning.

By enabling observations collected by different people, at different times, and in different places to be combined meaningfully, these standards expand rather than constrain the range of scientific investigations that become possible.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The Role of Models

Models connect scientific claims with observations.

They are tools for reasoning rather than repositories of truth.

Investigators are encouraged to employ analytical approaches appropriate to their questions while making their assumptions, methods, and reasoning transparent.

Different models illuminate different aspects of the evidence.

The scientific program therefore encourages comparison among competing approaches rather than commitment to any single inferential framework.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The Role of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence is treated as a scientific assistant rather than an answer generator.

Participants are encouraged to use AI to:

- critique experimental designs;
- identify potential sources of bias;
- propose alternative hypotheses;
- suggest additional observations;
- compare analytical approaches;
- improve scientific writing and communication.

AI should broaden scientific thinking rather than replace participant observation or judgment.

The primary evidence always remains the observations themselves.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Scientific Communication

Scientific investigations conclude not with an answer, but with an argument.

Participants are encouraged to communicate:

- the claim under investigation;
- the observations collected;
- the methods employed;
- the reasoning connecting evidence to conclusions;
- uncertainties and limitations;
- opportunities for future investigation.

Communication is not the final stage of science.

It is one of the mechanisms by which scientific understanding develops.

Ideas become stronger through explanation, criticism, revision, and replication.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Community Science

Every observation contributes to something larger than an individual investigation.

Participants are not merely consumers of scientific knowledge.

They are contributors to a growing body of evidence that future participants may analyze in ways not yet imagined.

The scientific program therefore values careful documentation, reproducibility, and openness not only because they improve individual investigations, but because they enable collaboration across time, geography, and experience.

A carefully recorded observation may become important long after it is collected.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Evolution of the Program

The scientific program is intended to evolve.

New instruments, new observational capabilities, larger datasets, and new analytical methods will enable increasingly ambitious investigations.

The structure of the program should remain stable even as its content grows.

Participants should always be free to ask new questions, devise new observational strategies, and contribute new forms of evidence.

The scientific program should grow through the creativity of its community rather than through the accumulation of prescribed exercises.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Enduring Principle

Kepler does not teach conclusions.

It teaches the process by which conclusions are earned.

The project's success is measured not by whether participants reproduce accepted scientific ideas, but by whether they learn to construct careful observations, reason honestly from evidence, communicate uncertainty with clarity, and remain willing to revise their conclusions when confronted with new observations.

Science is not a destination.

It is a disciplined conversation between observation and explanation.

Kepler exists to invite more people into that conversation.

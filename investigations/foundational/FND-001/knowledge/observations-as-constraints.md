# Observations Are Constraints

## Purpose

One of the central ideas of Kepler is that observations are not measurements of celestial coordinates. They are measurements that constrain the possible structure of the sky.

This distinction fundamentally changes how observations from different instruments are understood and combined.

---

# From Coordinates to Constraints

A natural first assumption is that each observation should produce a celestial coordinate pair such as Right Ascension and Declination.

This is rarely true.

Instead, each observation contributes evidence about the unknown position of one or more celestial objects.

The celestial coordinates are inferred only after many observations are considered together.

Coordinates are therefore not primitive observations.

They are the result of inference.

---

# Examples

Different instruments contribute different kinds of constraints.

## Cross-staff

A cross-staff measures the angular separation between two celestial objects.

The observation states:

> The angular separation between object A and object B was measured to be a particular value.

If the position of one object is uncertain, the observation constrains both objects simultaneously.

---

## Quadrant

A quadrant measures altitude.

The observation states:

> At a particular time and observing location, this object had a measured altitude.

This constrains the object's possible position on the celestial sphere.

---

## Compass

A compass measures azimuth.

The observation states:

> At a particular time and observing location, this object had a measured azimuth.

Like altitude, this is not a coordinate.

It is another geometric constraint.

---

# Equality of Observation Types

Once observations are viewed as constraints, the apparent differences between instruments become much smaller.

A cross-staff observation is not fundamentally more or less valuable than an altitude measurement.

An altitude measurement is not fundamentally more or less valuable than an azimuth measurement.

Each contributes a different constraint on the same latent sky.

The scientific value of an observation depends not on its instrument class, but on the information it contributes when combined with other observations.

---

# Building an Observer's Atlas

The Observer's Atlas is therefore not constructed by independently determining celestial coordinates for each observation.

Instead, it is built by combining many independent constraints into a single coherent model of the sky.

Repeated observations strengthen existing constraints.

Different instrument classes contribute complementary information.

Additional observers improve confidence while revealing systematic differences among instruments and observers.

As observations accumulate, the inferred atlas becomes increasingly well supported.

---

# Consequences

Viewing observations as constraints has several important implications.

* Instruments are unified by the constraints they produce rather than by their physical construction.
* Multiple observation types naturally coexist within a single observational framework.
* Different inferential methods may operate on the same observational dataset without changing the observations themselves.
* New instrument classes can be incorporated by defining the constraints they contribute rather than inventing new coordinate systems.
* Coordinate pairs become inferred properties of the sky rather than direct products of individual observations.

---

# Relationship to Kepler

Kepler deliberately separates three ideas.

* **Observation** is the collection of evidence.
* **Inference** combines that evidence into a model.
* **Scientific understanding** emerges from comparing competing models against the accumulated observations.

This separation allows the same observations to support many different inferential approaches while preserving the original evidence from which every conclusion is derived.

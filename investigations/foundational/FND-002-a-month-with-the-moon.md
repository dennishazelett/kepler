# FND-002 — A Month With the Moon

## Summary

The Moon is the most dynamic object in the naked-eye sky.

The “finger pointing at the moon” is a Buddhist metaphor found in Mahāyāna texts such as the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra and later associated with Chan/Zen, where it warns against mistaking teachings for the truth they point to. Kepler adopts the same philosophy. Instruments, diagrams, textbooks, software, and even these investigation sheets are only guides. They are valuable only insofar as they lead us back to careful observation of the natural world. This investigation begins by looking at the Moon itself.

Unlike the stars, it changes its appearance from night to night, moves rapidly relative to the background stars, and is frequently absent from the sky altogether. These characteristics make it an ideal subject for developing the habits of repeated scientific observation.

This investigation invites participants to follow the Moon through a complete lunation, recording what they observe without attempting to explain it in advance. The objective is not to learn the accepted description of lunar phases, but to construct an observational record from which patterns and questions naturally emerge.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Scientific Question

> **How does the Moon change over the course of a lunation?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Objectives

By completing this investigation, participants should develop experience in:

- making repeated observations over an extended period;
- maintaining a consistent observational record;
- distinguishing direct observations from interpretation;
- recognizing patterns that emerge only through longitudinal observation;
- reasoning about incomplete datasets caused by weather or observing conditions.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Investigation

Observe the Moon as often as practical throughout a complete lunation.

For each observation, record as much information as is reasonably possible. Not every observation will contain every piece of information, and some observing opportunities will inevitably be lost due to weather or other circumstances.

The emphasis is on producing an honest observational record rather than a perfect one.

Participants are encouraged to sketch the Moon whenever practical. Sketches often preserve details that are difficult to describe in words and provide valuable opportunities for comparison as the investigation progresses.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Suggested Observations

Examples of observations include:

- date and time;
- observing location;
- observing conditions;
- whether the Moon was visible;
- sketch of the Moon;
- description of the illuminated portion;
- orientation of the illuminated limb;
- approximate position in the sky;
- nearby stars or constellations;
- optional angular measurements using an approved Kepler instrument.

These observations are suggestions rather than requirements.

Investigators should record whatever observations they consider scientifically useful.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Scientific Products

This investigation contributes permanently to the participant's scientific record.

Products may include:

- a complete lunation observing log;
- a collection of dated lunar sketches;
- new pages within the Observer's Atlas;
- the participant's first longitudinal observational dataset;
- a growing archive of lunar observations that may be extended across many future lunations.

The value of these products increases as additional lunations are observed over months and years.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Reflection

At the conclusion of the investigation, review the complete observational record.

Rather than asking only what has been learned, consider:

- What patterns became apparent only after many observations?
- Which observations were surprising?
- Which observations remain unexplained?
- What additional observations would strengthen the investigation?
- What new scientific questions emerged?

The purpose of the investigation is not simply to document the Moon, but to experience how careful observation produces increasingly interesting scientific questions.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Relationship to Other Investigations

The observational habits developed here support many later investigations, including studies of planetary motion, elongation, retrograde motion, and historical scientific claims.

The observations collected during this investigation also become a permanent component of the participant's Observer's Atlas and may be revisited in future investigations.

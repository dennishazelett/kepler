# Purpose

Every scientific investigation in Kepler begins with the same question:

> **Where is everything?**

This investigation invites you to answer that question by constructing your own atlas of the night sky.

Unlike a commercial star chart, your atlas is not something you purchase or download. It is built gradually from your own observations. Over weeks, months, and eventually years, it becomes a personal scientific record of your relationship with the sky.

This is the signature foundational investigation of Kepler.

Nearly every later investigation—from following the Sun, to tracking the planets, to evaluating historical scientific claims—builds upon the atlas you begin here.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Learning Objectives

By completing this investigation you should:

- become familiar with the major features of the night sky;
- learn one common astronomical coordinate system;
- practice making repeatable astronomical measurements;
- understand how repeated observations accumulate into scientific knowledge;
- create a scientific artifact that will support future investigations.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Background

One of the first problems faced by every astronomer is representation.

The sky is a three-dimensional sphere surrounding the observer, yet observations must somehow be recorded, shared, and compared.

Astronomers have developed many coordinate systems and map projections for this purpose.

For this investigation, Kepler adopts the **equatorial coordinate system** (Right Ascension and Declination). It is widely used by both professional and amateur astronomers because it provides a stable way to describe the positions of stars independent of the observer's location on Earth.

You are **not** expected to understand all of the geometry immediately.

Instead, you will gradually become familiar with the coordinate system by using it.

# Your Task

Begin constructing your own Observer's Atlas.

The atlas should grow entirely from your own observations.

It does not need to be complete.

In fact, it should never really be considered finished.

Every future investigation may add new observations, corrections, annotations, and discoveries.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Suggested Workflow

These steps are intended as guidance rather than rigid instructions.

## 1. Become familiar with the coordinate grid

Examine the blank atlas.

Notice:

- the celestial equator;
- the declination grid;
- the right ascension grid.

Spend a few observing sessions simply relating the printed coordinate system to the real sky.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. Choose a small region of sky

Do not attempt to map everything.

Instead, begin with a recognizable group of bright stars.

A small, carefully observed map is more valuable than an ambitious but incomplete one.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. Observe repeatedly

Return to the same region on several nights.

Notice:

- which stars are easy to relocate;
- which are easily confused;
- how your confidence changes with experience.

Record every observing session.

Time matters.

A star observed at different times of night occupies different positions in your local sky while retaining the same position in the equatorial coordinate system.

Recognizing this distinction is one of the central lessons of the investigation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. Measure relationships

Use your instruments to measure the sky.

Examples include:

- angular separation between nearby stars;
- altitude measurements;
- repeated observations on different nights.

Your measurements should help you understand and verify the structure of your atlas rather than simply decorate it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5. Populate the atlas

Gradually add:

- bright stars;
- star names (when known);
- observational notes;
- measured angular relationships;
- uncertainty;
- revisions.

Do not erase mistakes.

Scientific understanding often develops through revision.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 6. Revisit earlier observations

Return to regions you mapped weeks earlier.

Ask yourself:

- Would I place this star differently today?
- Were my earlier measurements reasonable?
- Which parts of the atlas inspire the greatest confidence?
- Which parts deserve additional observations?

Your atlas should evolve.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Suggestions

You may find it helpful to:

- begin with the brightest stars;
- work outward gradually;
- observe from the same location initially;
- compare measurements made on different nights;
- develop your own notation for uncertainty;
- annotate the atlas with questions as well as answers.

There is no expectation that every participant's atlas will look the same.

# A Living Scientific Record

Your Observer's Atlas is not a reference chart.

It is a living scientific record of your own observations and your current understanding of the sky.

Every object in your atlas should be supported by one or more observations.

As you return to the same stars over weeks, months, and years, those observations accumulate. Your understanding of each object's position becomes better supported, your confidence grows, and your atlas gradually evolves.

The stars themselves do not move appreciably over the course of this investigation.

Your understanding of them does.

Unlike a commercial star atlas, your Observer's Atlas preserves the history of that understanding.

You are encouraged to revisit objects many times as well as trying to observe as many different objects as possible. Repeated observations reveal measurement uncertainty, improve confidence, and create a richer scientific record. New observations broaden your understanding of the sky.Your atlas is therefore not simply a map of the sky.

It is also a map of your own journey as an observer.

# Further Exploration

The equatorial coordinate system is only one way to represent the sky.

Astronomers have developed many others.

If you are interested in the mathematics of celestial cartography, consider exploring topics such as:

- stereographic projection;
- gnomonic projection;
- equal-area projections;
- celestial globes;
- historical star atlases.

These topics are not required for this investigation, but they provide fascinating examples of how mathematical ideas shape scientific practice.

# Closing Thoughts

An astronomer does not learn the sky in a single evening.

An atlas is built one observation at a time.

Years from now, your Observer's Atlas should contain not only the positions of stars, but a record of how your own understanding of the sky developed.

Every mark on its pages represents a moment spent looking carefully at the universe.

That habit of careful observation is the true objective of this investigation.
